## The short answer

Setting up a planted aquarium is a sequence, and doing it in order saves you a lot of grief. Pick a tank and a light suited to the plants you want. Lay a substrate that feeds roots. Build your hardscape of stone and wood before any water goes in. Plant heavily while the tank is shallow-filled. Fill it slowly, get the filter and heater running, and then [cycle it](/aquarium-cycling) for three to six weeks before a single fish arrives. Add livestock last, and slowly. The two mistakes that sink most first tanks are rushing the cycle and planting too sparsely, so plant thickly and be patient.

## 1. Choose the tank and the gear

Bigger tanks are more stable and more forgiving than nano tanks, so if you have the space, a 60 to 120 litre tank is an easier first project than a tiny one. Whatever the size, you need four bits of equipment.

- **Light** matched to your plants. Easy plants want modest light; carpets and reds want more. See [aquarium lighting](/equipment/lighting) for the PAR bands.
- **Filter** rated for your volume, aiming for a turnover of four to six times the tank per hour. See [filtration](/equipment/filtration).
- **Heater** at roughly one to one and a half watts per litre. See [heaters](/equipment/heaters).
- A **liquid test kit** for ammonia, nitrite and nitrate. Non-negotiable, it is how you read the cycle.

CO2 is optional. Decide now whether you are building a [low-tech or high-tech tank](/guides/low-tech-vs-high-tech-planted-tank), because it changes which plants you choose.

## 2. Lay the substrate

The substrate is the decision that shapes the whole tank, so it is worth getting right the first time. For a planted first tank, an active [aquasoil](/substrates) feeds the roots and gently softens the water for about a year, which suits most plants and shrimp. Inert sand or gravel works too, as long as you push [root tabs](/glossary) in for heavy root feeders. Slope it up toward the back, deeper at the rear than the front, which gives the scape depth and helps the design.

## 3. Build the hardscape

Do this before any real water goes in, so you can move heavy stone and wood freely. Arrange your [stones and wood](/hardscape) with the composition in mind: an off-centre focal point, an odd number of rocks, and open space to breathe. Our [aquascaping design guide](/aquascaping-design) covers the rule of thirds and the layout shapes. Remember that some stones raise pH and hardness and most wood leaches tannins, so choose hardscape that suits the water you want.

## 4. Plant it, heavily

Fill the tank just an inch or two above the substrate first. Damp and shallow keeps the soil in place and the plants easy to see while you work. Now plant, and plant more than feels necessary, because a heavily planted tank out-competes algae from day one.

Group plants by where they go: carpets and low plants at the front, mid-height species in the middle, tall stems and big leaves at the back. Our [how to plant an aquarium](/guides/how-to-plant-an-aquarium) guide covers the technique for each plant type. For a first tank, lean on hardy species.

Epiphytes like anubias and java fern get glued or tied to hardscape, never buried. Rosette plants like crypts go into the substrate. See our [best beginner plants](/guides/best-beginner-aquarium-plants) list for the full hardy shortlist.

## 5. Fill it and start the equipment

Top the tank up slowly, pouring onto a plate, your hand or the hardscape so you do not blast the substrate into a cloud. Add a dechlorinator to neutralise the tap water's chlorine and chloramine, which would otherwise kill your future filter bacteria. Turn on the filter, the heater and the light on a timer (start at six to eight hours a day), and let it run.

## 6. Cycle the tank

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that kills fish. A new tank has no bacteria to process the ammonia that fish produce, so you grow that colony first. Read the full method in [how to cycle a tank](/aquarium-cycling). In short: add an ammonia source, seed it with bacteria, and test every few days until the tank clears an ammonia dose to zero ammonia and zero nitrite in 24 hours. In a heavily planted tank the plants absorb much of the ammonia, softening the whole process.

Watch the numbers with your test kit and get familiar with what [each reading means](/water-chemistry). Some early [algae](/algae) and a little plant [melt](/guides/why-are-my-aquarium-plants-melting) are normal in a new tank, not a crisis.

## 7. Stock it, slowly

Once the tank is cycled, add livestock a few at a time so the bacteria colony can grow into the new load. Start with hardy species and use the [tank planner](/planner) and [compatibility tool](/compatibility) to check everything gets along and fits your water.

A shrimp or snail cleanup crew earns its place early. From here it is mostly a [maintenance routine](/aquarium-maintenance): weekly water changes, a glass clean, and trimming as the plants fill in.

## Common first-tank mistakes

- **Rushing the cycle.** Adding fish to an uncycled tank is the single most common cause of early losses. Wait for zero ammonia and zero nitrite.
- **Planting too sparsely.** Empty space is an invitation to algae. Plant thickly from the start.
- **Too much light, too soon.** Long, bright photoperiods in a young tank feed algae faster than plants. Start at six to eight hours.
- **Chasing demanding plants without CO2.** Carpets and reds without CO2 melt and hand their light to algae. Match plants to your setup.
- **Overstocking and overfeeding.** Both spike waste faster than a new tank can handle. Go slow and feed lightly.
