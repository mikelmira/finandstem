## The short answer

Adult neon tetras and adult cherry shrimp coexist in a planted tank without trouble. The pairing has one specific failure mode: newly hatched shrimplets sit inside a neon tetra's prey-size window and get eaten if they cannot find cover. If you are not trying to grow a shrimp colony, this is a fine combination. If you want the shrimp population to multiply, you need dense moss cover for the babies, or you swap the neons for a less predatory fish like otocinclus. Stocking ratio matters too. Keep neon density on the lower side of normal and shrimp density on the higher side, and both populations can run alongside each other indefinitely.

## What the science says

Adult neon tetras (*Paracheirodon innesi*) reach about 3.5 to 4cm in the aquarium ([FishBase](https://www.fishbase.se/summary/Paracheirodon-innesi)). They are micropredators in the wild. Their natural diet is small invertebrates, insect larvae, and anything tiny enough to fit in the mouth. An adult cherry shrimp (*Neocaridina davidi*) at 2 to 2.5cm is well above that threshold and registers as scenery rather than food.

Shrimplets are a different story. A freshly hatched *Neocaridina davidi* is around 1 to 1.5mm and looks like a slow-moving piece of debris. That is exactly what neons eat. There is no behavioural switch you can flip to teach the fish to ignore them. Predation is the default response.

The good news is that shrimp colony dynamics work on overwhelming numbers. A bonded female cherry shrimp produces 20 to 30 eggs per clutch every 30 days. Even in a tank with mild predation pressure, enough shrimplets survive in dense moss to keep the colony stable. In a tank with heavy cover and a moderate neon school, the colony will grow.

I have run neons and cherries separately for long enough to know what each species does on its own, and I have tried the combination twice in a single tank. The first attempt was a 40-litre setup with six neons added to an established cherry colony of around 30 adults. Within two months the visible shrimplet count dropped to near zero. The adult shrimp were untouched. I moved the neons out and the colony recovered inside three breeding cycles.

The second attempt worked. Same fish count, same shrimp count, but a wall of Java moss covering one full piece of driftwood and a thick layer of floating Salvinia on top to dim the light. The colony grew through the predation pressure. Not as fast as it would have without fish, but it grew. The lesson I took from the two runs: the shrimplets need somewhere the neons cannot reach, and that means moss volume, not moss decoration.

## The shrimplet survival trick

Cover and food are the two variables that decide whether your colony grows.

Cover means dense moss, ideally <SpeciesCard slug="java-moss" /> attached to wood or stone and left to fill in for two to three months before adding fish. Aim for a moss volume of around 10 to 20 percent of the tank by visual estimate. Shrimplets spend their first weeks inside the moss, grazing biofilm off the fronds and only leaving when they are too big to be neon food.

Food means biofilm and fine particulate. Cherry shrimp eat what falls past the fish, the algae that grows on hardscape, and anything biological that accumulates over time. You do not need to feed the shrimp directly in a planted tank with a moderate fish bioload. Their food is the leftovers.

The other variable is flow. Strong directional current pushes shrimplets out into open water where the neons can pick them off. A sponge filter or a gentle canister return with a spray bar works. Avoid powerheads.

## Better fish for a dedicated shrimp colony

If the shrimp colony is the goal and the fish are secondary, three species do better than neons:

<SpeciesCard slug="otocinclus" />

Otocinclus completely ignore shrimplets. They graze biofilm off the same surfaces the shrimp do, both species occupy similar parameter ranges (20 to 26 degrees Celsius, soft to moderate water, pH 6 to 7.5), and the visual pairing of orange shrimp and small grey grazing catfish is striking. The only requirement is a tank with biofilm already established (run it for at least three months before adding otos).

<SpeciesCard slug="pygmy-corydoras" />

Pygmy corys are the second pick. They swim in mid-water shoals (unusual for the genus), top out at 2.5cm, and only target food once it hits the substrate. They occasionally take a shrimplet but the rate is far lower than with neons.

<SpeciesCard slug="sparkling-gourami" />

A trio of sparkling gouramis adds a centrepiece without the school pressure. They take some shrimplets but they hunt slowly and inefficiently. A well-covered tank with sparkling gouramis routinely produces growing shrimp colonies.

## Stocking ratios that work

For a planted tank with both species, these numbers work in practice. Adjust down if your tank is heavily stocked with other fish.

- **40 litre tank:** 8 neon tetras and a starter colony of 10 to 15 cherry shrimp. Add an extra wad of Java Moss before introducing fish.
- **60 litre tank:** 10 to 12 neons and 15 to 20 shrimp. Plant heavily, run a sponge filter.
- **100 litre tank:** 15 to 20 neons and a colony of 30+ shrimp. At this volume, shrimplets have enough refuge that the colony grows visibly month over month.

## What about other tetras?

The same logic applies to every nano tetra at different intensity levels.

<SpeciesCard slug="cardinal-tetra" />

Cardinal tetras are slightly bigger than neons (4 to 5cm) and slightly more predatory on shrimplets. The pairing works but with a higher cover requirement. See the [neon vs cardinal comparison](/guides/neon-tetra-vs-cardinal-tetra) for the rest of the differences.

<SpeciesCard slug="ember-tetra" />

Ember tetras are smaller (2cm) and only take the smallest shrimplets. The pairing with cherries is the most shrimp-friendly of the tetra options. See [How many ember tetras should you keep together](/guides/how-many-ember-tetras-to-keep-together) for the schooling math.

<SpeciesCard slug="chili-rasbora" />

Chili rasboras are even smaller (under 2cm) and almost never take shrimplets. If you want a colourful nano school in a dedicated shrimp tank, this is the safest fish in the catalogue. The trade-off is parameters: chilis want soft, acidic, blackwater conditions that cherries tolerate but do not love.
