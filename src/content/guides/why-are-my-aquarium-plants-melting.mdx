## The short answer

Melting plants are usually not dying, they are converting. Most aquarium plants are farmed **emersed**, grown in air on a nursery, and when they go underwater they shed those air-grown leaves and grow new submerged ones. This "conversion melt" is normal and temporary. Cryptocorynes take it further with dramatic "crypt melt", dropping every leaf then regrowing from the roots. In both cases, keep the roots and rhizome intact, trim off the mush, and wait, new growth follows in two to four weeks. Melting only signals a real problem when it hits **established** plants, in which case you look for a trigger.

## Conversion melt: the normal kind

Here is the thing nobody tells beginners: the plant you buy was almost certainly grown [emersed](/glossary), out of water, in a nursery, because plants grow faster and cleaner in humid air. Its leaves are built for air. When you submerge it, those leaves cannot cope and dissolve, while the plant grows a fresh set of underwater leaves from the base. So a brand-new plant losing leaves in its first weeks is doing exactly what it should.

<SpeciesCard slug="rotala-rotundifolia" />

What to do: nothing dramatic. Trim off any leaves that have gone soft, transparent or slimy so they do not rot, leave the roots and any healthy growth, and be patient. New, tank-adapted growth appears within a few weeks and the plant takes off from there.

## Crypt melt: alarming but harmless

Cryptocorynes have a party trick: after being planted, moved, or hit with a change in water parameters, they often drop **every leaf** almost overnight, leaving bare stems and roots. This is crypt melt, and it fools countless beginners into binning a perfectly healthy plant.

<SpeciesCard slug="cryptocoryne-wendtii" />

The roots survive. Leave the plant exactly where it is, and it regrows a complete set of leaves from the crown within two to four weeks, often better adapted than before. The one rule: do not pull it up "to check", which just resets the clock.

## When melt is a real problem

Established plants that suddenly melt, not new arrivals, are telling you something changed. Work through the likely triggers:

- **A parameter swing.** A big shift in temperature, pH, hardness or TDS, often from a large or careless water change, can trigger a melt. See [water chemistry](/water-chemistry).
- **Low or unstable CO2.** Demanding plants stall and melt when [CO2](/equipment/co2-injection) drops or swings. Carpets like HC feel this first.

<SpeciesCard slug="hc-cuba" />

- **A nutrient deficiency.** Yellowing, holes or pale new growth before the melt point at a shortage. Diagnose it in the [deficiency hub](/deficiencies).
- **Too little light.** A plant slowly starved of light thins and melts from the bottom.
- **A chemical hit.** An algaecide, a copper medication, or an excess-dose of liquid carbon can burn sensitive plants and mosses. Check what went in recently.

## What not to do

- **Don't rip out melting plants.** The roots are usually alive and regrowing.
- **Don't panic-change all the water.** A big swing can make melt worse, not better. Keep conditions stable.
- **Don't ignore the mush.** Rotting leaves foul the water and feed [algae](/algae). Trim them out.
