## The short answer

A 30 litre planted tank is a nano. The honest stocking math is one schooling species, kept in numbers, plus a shrimp colony. The four fish that fit best are the chili rasbora, ember tetra, celestial pearl danio, and pygmy corydoras. All four sit under 3cm as adults, have low bioloads, and display proper schooling behaviour in groups of eight to ten. Neon tetras do not belong here even though pet stores will sell them to you for a tank this size. They need a 60 litre minimum to show natural behaviour and the bioload at the upper end of acceptable. The plant side is easier: any low-light, no-CO2 plant works. Three sample stockings with full plant lists are below.

## Why 30 litres is one-school territory

The bioload math is straightforward. A 30 litre planted tank has roughly 25 litres of usable water after substrate and hardscape. The accepted stocking guideline for nano fish is about one centimetre of adult fish per litre of water in a planted tank, which gives you a 25cm fish budget.

A school of 10 ember tetras at 2cm each consumes 20cm of that budget. Add eight ember tetras instead and you have room for a small bottom group or a shrimp colony. Add a school of 10 neon tetras at 3.5cm each and you are at 35cm, well over budget, plus the species needs more swimming space than 30 litres provides.

The footprint argument matters too. A standard 30 litre cube is around 30cm long. Schooling fish need length to swim. Neons in a short tank will reach the end wall and turn back constantly, which they read as a stress signal. Smaller fish are unbothered by the same dimensions.

## The four top picks

### Chili rasbora (*Boraras brigittae*)

<SpeciesCard slug="chili-rasbora" />

The best species-appropriate match for a 30 litre planted tank. Chilis come from peat swamps in southwest Borneo and want soft, acidic, tannin-stained water. A nano tank planted with floating plants and dosed with catappa leaves is exactly their environment. Keep 10 to 15 of them. They sit mid-water, school loosely, and pop a deep glowing red against dark substrate. The trade-off is parameter discipline. If your tap water is hard, you need RO water or a different fish.

### Ember tetra (*Hyphessobrycon amandae*)

<SpeciesCard slug="ember-tetra" />

The most forgiving option. Ember tetras tolerate a wide parameter range, ship well, and the fluorescent orange-red colour is visible across the room against green plants and dark wood. Keep 10. They will school loosely in mid-water and breed casually in a mature tank without intervention. See [how many ember tetras should you keep together](/guides/how-many-ember-tetras-to-keep-together) for the schooling depth-of-detail.

### Celestial pearl danio (*Danio margaritatus*)

<SpeciesCard slug="celestial-pearl-danio" />

The cool-water option. CPDs come from a small area of Myanmar's Shan State at altitude, in hard and slightly alkaline water. They want temperatures below 25 degrees C and pH 7 to 7.5, which is the opposite of the blackwater nano profile. Pick CPDs if your tap water is hard and your house runs cool. Six to eight in a well-planted nano show stunning male display behaviour. Keep two females per male to stop the males stressing each other.

### Pygmy corydoras (*Corydoras pygmaeus*)

<SpeciesCard slug="pygmy-corydoras" />

The unusual choice. Unlike most corys which stay glued to the substrate, *Corydoras pygmaeus* swims in mid-water shoals of six to eight. The behaviour is rare and pretty. They need fine sand substrate (gravel damages the barbels) and they ignore shrimp entirely. They tolerate the same parameters as ember tetras, which makes for a tempting two-species tank, except that mixing two schools in 30 litres usually means neither school is big enough. If you pick pygmy corys, run a school of eight as the only fish.

## The shrimp partner

Every one of the four fish above tolerates a cherry shrimp colony in the same tank. The shrimplet predation rate varies by fish:

<SpeciesCard slug="cherry-shrimp" />

- **Chili rasbora:** very low predation. They are too small to eat all but the smallest shrimplets.
- **Ember tetra:** low predation. Adults take occasional shrimplets but the colony grows.
- **Celestial pearl danio:** low-to-moderate predation. CPDs are slightly more predatory than the tetra species.
- **Pygmy corydoras:** essentially zero predation.

In all four cases, dense moss cover means the colony grows steadily. See [can neon tetras live with cherry shrimp](/guides/can-neon-tetras-live-with-cherry-shrimp) for the moss-coverage detail. Start with 10 to 15 shrimp; in six months the colony will be 30+ if the parameters are right.

## Plant pairings for the nano

Three plants do all the work in a 30 litre tank without CO2.

<SpeciesCard slug="java-moss" />

Java moss is the carpet substitute, the shrimplet nursery, and the cheap insurance plant. Tie a portion to a piece of driftwood and let it cover one corner of the tank. It will trap biofilm, hide shrimplets, and give the fish school somewhere to dart through.

<SpeciesCard slug="anubias-nana" />

Anubias nana is the hardscape accent. Glue the rhizome to a piece of wood or stone with cyanoacrylate; the plant lives off the water column and the slow growth means it almost never needs trimming. Place it in the midground at an angle that catches the light.

<SpeciesCard slug="cryptocoryne-wendtii" />

Cryptocoryne wendtii is the substrate plant. Use it to fill the midground around the hardscape. It will melt the first two weeks after planting (a famous quirk of the genus) and regrow stronger from the roots. Push a root tab next to it every four months for best colour.

For a full list of low-tech plants that work in this tank, see [low-light aquarium plants that don't need CO2](/guides/low-light-aquarium-plants-no-co2).

## What not to put in a 30 litre

- **Neon tetras and cardinal tetras.** Both species need 60 litres minimum to school properly. They are sold for smaller tanks all the time, but the fish are stressed in that footprint.
- **Bettas in a community.** A male betta in 30 litres is fine alone. The moment you add tetras or shrimp, the betta either harasses them or gets fin-nipped. The combination almost never works as billed.
- **Dwarf gouramis.** A 30 litre tank is too small for a dwarf gourami's territorial range. They become listless or aggressive.
- **Goldfish.** Always. A single fancy goldfish needs at least 75 litres. A common goldfish needs a pond.
- **Otocinclus.** Six-plus otos in 30 litres is technically possible but they need a heavily mature biofilm-rich tank, which a 30 litre rarely achieves. Skip otos at this volume.

## Sample stockings

Three concrete builds. Costs are rough USD estimates from typical retail.

### The blackwater nano

Goal: a soft-water Southeast Asian biotope.

- 12 chili rasboras (~$50)
- 15 cherry shrimp (~$45)
- Plants: Java moss, Anubias nana, Cryptocoryne wendtii (~$25)
- Hardscape: spider wood, dark sand, three catappa leaves (~$25)

**Total stocking cost:** ~$145

### The colour-pop community

Goal: high visibility, easy to maintain.

- 10 ember tetras (~$35)
- 15 cherry shrimp (~$45)
- Plants: Java moss, Anubias nana, Cryptocoryne wendtii, a single Hygrophila polysperma stem cluster (~$30)
- Hardscape: dark stones, dark sand (~$20)

**Total stocking cost:** ~$130

### The cool-water Myanmar

Goal: an unusual species in a different parameter profile.

- 8 celestial pearl danios (~$60)
- 15 cherry shrimp (~$45)
- Plants: Java moss, Anubias nana, Vallisneria spiralis for the back (~$30)
- Hardscape: light substrate, slate stones (~$20)

**Total stocking cost:** ~$155
