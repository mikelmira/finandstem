## The short answer

Red plants turn red as a defence against strong light, so the number one lever for colour is **light intensity**, not iron, whatever the marketing says. Strong light drives the red pigments; [CO2](/equipment/co2-injection) lets the plant handle that strong light without stalling; and iron plus a full [micronutrient](/deficiencies/iron-deficiency) supply, often with slightly lean nitrogen, lets the colour fully express. Get light and CO2 right first, keep [iron](/deficiencies) and trace topped up, run nitrate a touch lean, and reds glow. Get them wrong and the same plant grows green. Start with a forgiving red like Ludwigia and work up to the demanding stunners.

## Why plants turn red

The red and orange pigments in aquarium plants (anthocyanins and carotenoids) act like sunscreen, protecting the leaf from strong light. A plant only bothers making them when the light is intense enough to need them. In dim light, the plant makes only green chlorophyll, because that is all it needs. So the same stem that is deep red under a bright fixture will grow plain green under a weak one. This is why "why are my red plants green" almost always comes down to light.

## The recipe for red

Colour is a stack, and each layer matters in order:

1. **Strong light.** This is the trigger. Without enough light reaching the plant, nothing else brings out red. See [lighting](/equipment/lighting) for PAR.
2. **CO2.** Strong light without CO2 just stalls the plant and grows [algae](/algae). CO2 lets the plant use the light it is given. This is why serious reds live in [high-tech tanks](/guides/low-tech-vs-high-tech-planted-tank).
3. **Iron and full trace.** Reds are hungry for [iron](/deficiencies/iron-deficiency) and micronutrients. Under-dose and the colour fades to yellow-green in the new growth. Dose a complete fertiliser or a dedicated trace mix.
4. **Lean nitrogen.** Running nitrate slightly low tips the balance toward red pigments in many species. Push it too far and you get pale, [deficient](/deficiencies/nitrogen-deficiency) old leaves, so it is a fine line, not a starvation diet.

## The easiest reds

Start here if you are new to red plants. These show colour without a perfect high-tech setup.

<SpeciesCard slug="ludwigia-repens" />

The Ludwigias are the forgiving reds: bronze-red even in medium light, deep red with strong light and CO2. A great first red.

<SpeciesCard slug="alternanthera-reineckii-mini" />

Alternanthera reineckii 'Mini' brings a pink-red, compact and relatively hardy, a superb midground splash of colour.

<SpeciesCard slug="red-tiger-lotus" />

The red tiger lotus is a bulb plant that throws up striking red-and-green leaves, and it colours up well even in more modest tanks.

## The demanding stunners

<SpeciesCard slug="rotala-macrandra" />

<SpeciesCard slug="rotala-hra" />

Rotala macrandra ('Red') and Rotala 'H'ra' are the plants that make people fall in love with reds, and the ones that punish any weakness. They need strong light, stable CO2 and good dosing, and they are the first to pale and stunt if any of those slip. Grow the easy reds well before you reach for these.

## Troubleshooting

- **Red plants going green:** more light first, then check CO2. See [iron deficiency](/deficiencies/iron-deficiency) if new growth is pale.
- **Pale, washed-out new growth:** an iron or trace shortage. Dose a complete fertiliser.
- **Reds stunting or melting:** unstable or insufficient CO2, or light too strong for the CO2 you supply.
- **Old leaves yellowing while you chase colour:** you have pushed nitrogen too lean. Ease off.
